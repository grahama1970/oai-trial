Abstract

The rise of large language models (LLMs) has introduced new privacy challenges, particularly during inference where sensitive information in prompts may be exposed to proprietary LLM APIs. In this paper, we address the problem of formally protecting the sensitive information contained in a prompt while maintaining response quality. To this end, first, we introduce a cryptographically inspired notion of a prompt sanitizer which transforms an input prompt to protect its sensitive tokens. Second, we propose vel system that implements a prompt sanitizer. egorizes sensitive tokens into two types: (1) those where the LLM’s response depends solely on the format (such as SSNs, credit card numbers), for which we use format-preserving encryption (FPE); and (2) those where the response depends on specific values, (such as age, salary) for which we apply metric differential privacy (mDP). Our evaluation demonstrates that practical method to achieve meaningful privacy guarantees, while maintaining high utility compared to unsanitized prompts, and outperforming prior methods.

1 Introduction

The recent advent of large language models (LLMs) have brought forth a fresh set of challenges for protecting users’ data privacy. LLMs and their APIs present significant privacy concerns at inference time, which are fundamentally distinct from the well-documented risks of training data memorization [19, 50, 63, 95]. While the potential adversary in training data scenarios could be any API user, the threat during inference primarily stems from the model owner—typically the organization hosting the LLM. This inference stage poses a serious privacy threat, because prompts are expressed in terms of semantically rich natural language, potentially containing a substantial amount of sensitive information. This sensitive information is, in fact, multi-faceted, ranging from personally identifiable information, such as social security numbers (SSNs) or credit card numbers, to personal data, such as health or financial information.

The ensuing privacy threat is exacerbated with the growing use of in-context learning, that involves presenting the LLM with a few training examples as part of the prompt during inference [17]. This has shifted some of the concerns around privacy of training data from training time to inference time. Furthermore, the consumer-facing nature and widespread accessibility [32, 73, 60, 90, 58] of LLMs have significantly amplified the scope of these privacy risks. What renders the privacy risks particularly potent is the general lack of awareness among users, leading to unwitting disclosure of sensitive information [11]. Consequently, certain countries, such as Italy [22], along with financial institutions  [59, 99], government agencies [92, 74, 93], medical institutions  [33] as well as companies, such as Samsung [87, 86], Amazon [5] and Apple [8], have prohibited the use of propeitary LLMs altogether, underscoring the significance of these privacy concerns.

Prior work on privacy-preserving LLMs [7, 65, 53, 82, 89, 71, 100], has primarily focused on mechanisms during training. Unfortunately, these training-time mechanisms can only protect the (pre)-training data: data provided as part of the prompt poses additional privacy risks that are not addressed by these mechanisms [34]. While recent research has begun to address the privacy of prompts, solutions based on homomorphic encryption and secure multi-party computation [49, 26, 54] are computationally expensive in practice, with state-of-the-art techniques taking over 1616 minutes for a single inference on BERT [79]. More efficient solutions either lack formal privacy guarantees [66, 103], require changes to current LLM APIs [69] or make impractical design choices [88, 61, 27] (see Sec. 7 for more details).

To this end, we make the following contributions. First, we introduce a cryptographically inspired notion of a prompt sanitizer that takes a prompt and transforms it in a way that protects sensitive tokens yet still preserves the ability of the LLM to make a useful prediction. We provide a formal analysis of both its privacy and utility. Second, we propose system that instantiates a prompt sanitizer. We focus on the sensitive information that can derived solely from the individual tokens, specifically numeric or alphanumeric tokens. It is important to note that addressing this aspect is paramount as it poses the most immediate risk and represents a ‘‘low-hanging fruit’’ for potential adversaries. This is because an adversary can exploit the sensitive tokens (such as SSN, credit card number) independently, without needing to process or access additional context from the prompts. The task of handling privacy risks stemming from the contextual linguistic semantics11 1 This refers to cases where individual tokens may not be sensitive but, when considered in the context of the full prompt, could leak information because of the underlying natural language semantics. of the entire prompt [72, 15] is left as future work.

2 Background

Notation. Let V be the set of tokens that denotes the vocabulary of a language model and V∗\textsf{V}^{*} the set of possible strings over V (recall that a prompt and its response are strings over V). We represent a sequence of tokens 𝝈∈V∗\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in\textsf{V}^{*} with a boldface. Let ff be a LLM and 𝝆∈V∗\boldsymbol{\rho}\in\textsf{V}^{*} be a prompt for it. A prompt is a sequence of tokens from V, i.e., 𝝆=⟨σ1,⋯,σn⟩,σi∈V,∀i∈[n]\boldsymbol{\rho}=\langle\sigma_{1},\cdots,\sigma_{n}\rangle,\sigma_{i}\in\textsf{V},\forall i\in[n]. Let ℙ⁡(V)\mathbb{P}(\textsf{V}) denote the space of all probability distribution over V.

2.1 Language Model

Definition 1.

A language model ff is an auto-regressive model over a token vocabulary V. It works as a deterministic algorithm that takes as input a prompt 𝛒∈V∗\boldsymbol{\rho}\in\textsf{V}^{*} and tokens previously output by the model 𝛔∈V∗\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in\textsf{V}^{*}, and outputs a probability distribution p=f⁡(𝛒,𝛔)p=f(\boldsymbol{\rho},\boldsymbol{\sigma}) for p∈ℙ⁡(V)p\in\mathbb{P}(V).

A language model’s response to a prompt 𝝆\boldsymbol{\rho} is a random variable 𝝈∈V∗\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in\textsf{V}^{*} that is defined algorithmically as follows. We begin with an empty sequence of tokens 𝝈=⟨⟩\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\langle\rangle. As long as the last token in 𝝈\boldsymbol{\sigma} is not ⊥\bot (which we can be viewed as ”end of sequence” (EOS) token), we sample a token σ\sigma from the distribution f⁡(𝝆,𝝈)f(\boldsymbol{\rho},\boldsymbol{\sigma}) (using a decoding algorithm, such as standard multinomial sampling, or greedy sampling of the single most likely next token) and append it to 𝝈\boldsymbol{\sigma}. The algorithm stops once a special token ⊥\bot is emitted. Once the decoding algorithm is fixed, we can model ff as taking a prompt pp in V⋆\textsf{V}^{\star} and outputting a string in V⋆V^{\star}. In a slight abuse of notion, throughout the rest of the paper we will use f⁡(𝝆)f(\boldsymbol{\rho}) to denote the response string of the LLM on the input prompt 𝝆\boldsymbol{\rho}.

2.1.1 Tokens and Types

Given a sequence of tokens 𝝈∈V⋆\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in\textsf{V}^{\star}, a typed sequence is a 22-tuple 𝝈τ=⟨(σ1,τ1),⋯,(σn,τn)⟩\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\tau}=\langle(\sigma_{1},\tau_{1}),\cdots,(\sigma_{n},\tau_{n})\rangle, where τi∈T\tau_{i}\in\textsf{T} is the type of the substring σi\sigma_{i} of σ\sigma (we assume 𝝈=σ1⋅σ2⋯σn\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\sigma_{1}\cdot\sigma_{2}\cdots\sigma_{n}). Each type is associated with a domain. We also assume the existence of a type annotator.

Definition 2 (Type Annotator).

A type annotator is a deterministic algorithm ℳτ:V∗↦(V⋆×T)∗\mathcal{M}_{\tau}:\textsf{V}^{*}\mapsto(\textsf{V}^{\star}\times\textsf{T})^{*} that inputs a prompt 𝛒\boldsymbol{\rho} and outputs the corresponding typed sequence ⟨(σ1,τ1),⋯,(σn,τn)⟩\langle(\sigma_{1},\tau_{1}),\cdots,(\sigma_{n},\tau_{n})\rangle.

For example, consider the following prompt 𝝆\boldsymbol{\rho}:

“ Kaiser Soze is 50 years old and earns 500,000 per year. What is his ideal retirement plan?”

ℳτ​(𝝆)\mathcal{M}_{\tau}(\boldsymbol{\rho}) is given as follows:

“ (Kaiser Soze, [Name]) is (50, [Age]) years old and earns (500,000 , [Salary]) per year. What is his ideal retirement plan?”

where [Name], [Age], [Salary] are types of the tokens that precede it. For the ease of notation, here we only annotate tokens with sensitive types, i.e., all other non-annotated tokens have type ⟂\perp (which denotes non-sensitive token). Note that type annotation is context dependent. For example, consider the following two prompts: ρ1\rho_{1}= “My age is 53 years.” and ρ2\rho_{2}= “I stay at 53 Broadway Street.” The same token 5353 has two different types in the two prompts: type Age and type Street Number in ρ1\rho_{1} and ρ2\rho_{2}, respectively.

3 Prompt Sanitizer

Given an input prompt 𝝆\boldsymbol{\rho}, a prompt sanitizer (denoted by PS) transforms the entire prompt to a sanitized one 𝝆^\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}} with the goal of protecting the sensitive tokens contained in 𝝆\boldsymbol{\rho}. It is formally defined as follows:

Definition 3 (Prompt Sanitizer).

A prompt sanitizer PS=⟨S,ℳτ,E,D⟩\textsf{PS}=\langle\textsf{S},\mathcal{M}_{\tau},\textsf{E},\textsf{D}\rangle is a tuple of the following algorithms:

  • •

    Setup (S). The setup algorithm takes no input and outputs a secret key, as K←S\textsf{K}\leftarrow\textsf{S}.

  • •

    Type Annotator (ℳτ\mathcal{M}_{\tau}). The type annotator inputs a prompt (token sequence) 𝝆∈V∗\boldsymbol{\rho}\in\textsf{V}^{*} and outputs the corresponding type-annotated token sequence as 𝝆τ←ℳτ​(𝝆)\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\tau}\leftarrow\mathcal{M}_{\tau}(\boldsymbol{\rho}) (as defined in Def. 2).

  • •

    Sanitization (E)(\textsf{E}). The sanitization algorithm takes as input the secret key K and a type-annotated token sequence 𝝆τ∈(V⋆×T)∗\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\tau}\in(\textsf{V}^{\star}\times\textsf{T})^{*}. It outputs a token sequence 𝝆^∈V|𝝆τ|\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\in\textsf{V}^{|\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\tau}|}, as 𝝆^←E​(K,𝝆τ)\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\leftarrow\textsf{E}(\textsf{K},\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\tau}).

  • •

    Desanitization (D)(\textsf{D}). Desanitization takes a string (token sequence) 𝝊^∈V∗\hat{\boldsymbol{\upsilon}}\in\textsf{V}^{*} and processes it with the goal of reversing the effect of the sanitization algorithm, using the secret key K. This is represented as 𝝊←D​(K,𝝊^)\boldsymbol{\upsilon}\leftarrow\textsf{D}(\textsf{K},\hat{\boldsymbol{\upsilon}}) with 𝝊∈V|𝝊^|\boldsymbol{\upsilon}\in\textsf{V}^{|\hat{\boldsymbol{\upsilon}}|}.

Given a prompt 𝝆\boldsymbol{\rho}, the typical workflow of PS proceeds as follows:
(1)(1) type annotate the prompt to obtain 𝝆τ=ℳτ​(𝝆)\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\tau}=\mathcal{M}_{\tau}(\boldsymbol{\rho}),
(2)(2) sanitize the type annotated prompt using the secret key K as 𝝆^=E​(K,𝝆τ)\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}=\textsf{E}(\textsf{K},\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\tau}),
(3)(3) obtain the LLM’s response on the sanitized prompt as 𝝊^=f⁡(𝝆^)\hat{\boldsymbol{\upsilon}}=f(\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}),
(4)(4) desanitize the response to obtain 𝝊=D​(K,𝝊^)\boldsymbol{\upsilon}=\textsf{D}(\textsf{K},\hat{\boldsymbol{\upsilon}}).

In the above workflow, the desanitization algorithm restores information about the original prompt 𝝆\boldsymbol{\rho} in its output, 𝝊\boldsymbol{\upsilon}. In the special case where we run the desanitization algorithm directly on the sanitized prompt 𝝆^\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}} (which can be useful for instance if the PS is used to store a set of sensitive prompts on an untrusted platform for later use), we ideally expect 𝝊=𝝆\boldsymbol{\upsilon}=\boldsymbol{\rho}.

We require that the sanitization and desanitization algorithms are type preserving, which means that if 𝝆=⟨σ1,⋯,σn⟩\boldsymbol{\rho}=\langle\sigma_{1},\cdots,\sigma_{n}\rangle and 𝝆τ=⟨(σ1,τ1),⋯,(σn,τn)⟩←ℳτ​(𝝆)\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\tau}=\langle(\sigma_{1},\tau_{1}),\cdots,(\sigma_{n},\tau_{n})\rangle\leftarrow\mathcal{M}_{\tau}(\boldsymbol{\rho}) and 𝝆^←E​(K,𝝆τ)\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\leftarrow\textsf{E}(\textsf{K},\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\tau}) and ⟨(σ1′,τ1′),⋯,(σn′,τn′)⟩←ℳτ​(𝝆^)\langle(\sigma_{1}^{\prime},\tau_{1}^{\prime}),\cdots,(\sigma_{n}^{\prime},\tau_{n}^{\prime})\rangle\leftarrow\mathcal{M}_{\tau}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}) then it must be that (τ1,⋯,τn)=(τ1′,⋯,τn′)(\tau_{1},\cdots,\tau_{n})=(\tau_{1}^{\prime},\cdots,\tau_{n}^{\prime}).

3.1 Privacy Guarantee

The privacy game, denoted as 𝐆PS,ℒpp\mathbf{G}^{\mathrm{pp}}_{\textsf{PS},\mathcal{L}}, is designed to capture an adversary’s ability to distinguish between the sanitized outputs of two different prompts. In the game if the adversary picks two prompts that have a very different structure (e.g. different type or number of tokens), then the adversary can trivially distinguish between the corresponding sanitized prompts. To avoid these pathological cases, in the game we constrain the adversary to pick two prompts that have “similar structure”, which is formalized as a leakage function. Different instantiations of leakage function, which are discussed after the definition of the game, lead to different instantiations of the game.

In particular, the leakage function ℒ\mathcal{L} of a prompt sanitizer takes as input a prompt 𝝆\boldsymbol{\rho} and captures all the information about sensitive tokens that is leaked by 𝝆^=E​(𝝆,K)\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}=\textsf{E}(\boldsymbol{\rho},\textsf{K}), given a key K.

Intuitively, the above game implies that even after observing a sanitized prompt, an adversary should not be able to reliably differentiate between two prompts with the same leakage. The definition of the leakage function, ℒ\mathcal{L}, is crucial and depends on the the underlying sanitizer E. For example, if E sanitizes a token by redaction, for prompt 𝝆=\boldsymbol{\rho}=“My age is 26”, the leakage function outputs all the non-sensitive tokens, i.e., ℒ⁡(𝝆)\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\rho}) = “My age is [ ]”—this represents the minimal possible leakage as redaction is the strongest sanitization mechanism. Alternatively, if E encrypts sensitive tokens, ℒ\mathcal{L} might reveal the length of these tokens to 𝒜\mathcal{A}. Note that leakage function is a standard notion in cryptography [39]. For instance, for order-preserving encryption [68] the leakage function is essentially the numerical order to the input dataset.

Note that the restriction in the above game, requiring the pair of prompts to have the same leakage, aligns with standard notions in game-based cryptographic security definitions. For instance, this is analogous to the definition of security in order-preserving encryption (IND-FA-OCPA [68]), where the adversary is restricted to selecting pairs of data sequences that maintain the same order.

3.2 Utility Guarantee

Let 𝒬:V∗×V∗↦R𝒬\mathcal{Q}:\textsf{V}^{*}\times\textsf{V}^{*}\mapsto R_{\mathcal{Q}} be a quality oracle that evaluates the quality of a candidate response 𝝊\boldsymbol{\upsilon} for a prompt ρ\rho. Specifically, 𝒬⁡(𝝆,𝝊)\mathcal{Q}(\boldsymbol{\rho},\boldsymbol{\upsilon}) is a measure of the response’s goodness. Such a quality oracle has been used in prior work on LLMs [102].

Definition 4.

A prompt sanitizer PS satisfies (α,β)(\alpha,\beta) utility for a given prompt 𝛒∈V∗\boldsymbol{\rho}\in\textsf{V}^{*},

where the randomness is defined over both the LLM, f,  and PS.

The utility of the prompt sanitizer PS is evaluated by comparing the quality of the original response f⁡(𝝆)f(\boldsymbol{\rho}) with the one obtained through the PS pipeline. The above definition has two key characteristics. First, the utility is defined w.r.t to a specific prompt. This is because the quality of responses can vary significantly across different prompts. For example, consider the following prompt: 𝝆\boldsymbol{\rho} = “My age is 46. What is the average age of the population of New York?” Here, a high-quality LLM’s response should be invariant to the sensitive token (in this case, [Age]) in the prompt. This means that even after sanitization, we should be able to retrieve a correct and relevant response. On the other hand, for a conversational prompt used in a LLM-based chatbot to seek medical advice, the quality of the responses could vary significantly based on the specifics of the sanitization and de-sanitization algorithms of PS. Note that the quality oracle QQ can take various forms based on the type of the prompt. For instance, it might be a human evaluator who assigns a quality score, or it could be a predefined analytical expression in case the prompt has some special structures. Second, the utility is defined as an expectation since in general both the LLM f and the prompt sanitizer PS, are probabilistic. Note that when the distribution of f⁡(𝝆)f(\boldsymbol{\rho}) matches the distribution of D​(K,f​(𝝆^))\textsf{D}(\textsf{K},f(\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}})), this represents the strictest form of utility. If RQR_{Q} is a metric space with a distance metric dQd_{Q}, we can quantitatively measure the mean degradation in the quality of the response as dQ​(α,β)d_{Q}(\alpha,\beta).

4.1 Building Blocks

We start by describing the building blocks which will be used to sanitize the sensitive tokens.

4.1.1 Format-Preserving Encryption (FPE)

Under a format preserving encryption (FPE) scheme, the plaintext and the ciphertext has the same format. FPEs allow applications to process ciphertexts in the same way as the plaintexts and this backward compatibility makes them a popular tool for secure data analytics in practice. For instance, the ciphertext of a 16-digit credit card number encrypted under a FPE scheme would also be a 16-digit number33 3 One can add additional constraints, such as ensuring the last digit is the Luhn checksum or ensuring that the first four digit corresponds to a valid bank.; similarly the ciphertext of a date (say in the MM-DD-YYYY) would also be a valid date in the same format.

Definition 5 (Format Preserving Encryption (FPE)).

A format preserving encryption scheme is a tuple ℰ=⟨Gℱ,Eℱ,Dℱ⟩\mathcal{E}=\langle\textsf{G}_{\mathcal{F}},\textsf{E}_{\mathcal{F}},\textsf{D}_{\mathcal{F}}\rangle of polynomial time algorithms:

  • •

    Key Generation (Gℱ\textsf{G}_{\mathcal{F}}). The key generation algorithm is probabilistic polynomial time algorithm that takes as input a security parameter κ\kappa and outputs a secret key K as K←Gℱ​(1κ)\textsf{K}\leftarrow\textsf{G}_{\mathcal{F}}(1^{\kappa}).

  • •

    Encryption (Eℱ\textsf{E}_{\mathcal{F}})44 4 FPEs also take a tweak space as an input which we omit here for the ease of exposition. The encryption algorithm is deterministic polynomial time algorithm that takes as input a secret key K, a plaintext x∈ℳx\in\mathcal{M}, and a format N∈𝒩\textsf{N}\in\mathcal{N} and outputs a ciphertext y∈ℳy\in\mathcal{M} as y←Eℱ​(K,N,x)y\leftarrow\textsf{E}_{\mathcal{F}}(\textsf{K},\textsf{N},x).

Typically, the format of a plaintext is described as a finite set N over which the encryption function induces a permutation. For example, with SSNs this is the set of all nine-decimal-digit numbers.

4.1.2 Metric Local Differential Privacy (mLDP)

Differential privacy (DP) is a quantifiable measure of the stability of the output of a randomized mechanism to changes to its input. As a direct consequence of our threat model (Sec. 4.2), we work with the local model of DP (LDP) where each data point is individually randomized. Metric local differential privacy (mLDP) [23, 4, 85, 56] is a generalization of LDP which allows heterogenous guarantees for a pair of inputs based on a distance metric d⁡(⋅)d(\cdot) defined over the input space.

Definition 6 (Metric Local Differential Privacy (mLDP) [23]).

A randomized algorithm ℳ:𝒳→𝒴\mathcal{M}:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y} is ϵ\epsilon-mLDP for a given metric d:𝒳×𝒳↦ℤ≥0d:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\mapsto\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0} if for any pair of private values x,x′∈𝒳x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X} and any subset of output, 𝒪⊆𝒴\mathcal{O}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}

Pr[ℳ(x)∈𝒪]≤eϵ​d​(x,x′)⋅Pr[ℳ(x′)∈𝒪]\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}\big[\mathcal{M}(x)\in\mathcal{O}\big]\leq e^{\epsilon d(x,x^{\prime})}\cdot\mathrm{Pr}\big[\mathcal{M}(x^{\prime})\in\mathcal{O}\big] (3)

mLDP uses the distance between a pair of values to customize heterogeneous (different levels of) privacy guarantees for different pairs of private values. In particular, the privacy guarantee degrades linearly with the distance between a pair of data points. Intuitively, this means that only pairs of data points that are “close” to each other should be indistinguishable. Nevertheless. mLDP captures the privacy semantics of many real-world scenarios. For instance, let the input xx represent the annual revenue of a clothing firm. The information whether a firm is a top selling or a mid-range one is less sensitive than its actual revenue. Similarly, if xx corresponds to an age, whether a person is young or middle-aged is less sensitive than their actual age. 4.3 Design Goals

the following design goals.

  • •

    Formal Guarantees. uld be able to able to provide a formal privacy guarantee on the sanitized prompts.

  • •

    High Utility. We want ave high utility: i.e., the responses based on sanitized prompts should be “close” to the responses based on the original prompt.

  • •

    Stateless. Finally, the sanitization and de-sanitization process should be stateless: that is, uld not retain information (state) from any prior session. This design choice offers dual advantages. Firstly, storing sensitive information derived from users’ prompts/responses post-session termination would violate privacy and contravene legal frameworks, such as the EU’s GDPR [46] and California’s CCPA [18]. Additionally, these regulations grant individuals the Right to Deletion or Right to be Forgotten, allowing data owners to retract authorization previously granted for the use of their personal data. A stateful solution, unfortunately, would struggle to support the Right to Deletion without sacrificing de-sanitization capabilities. Secondly, a stateless solution offers flexibility and storage efficiency. To illustrate, consider the following two sequences of user actions:

    A1=⟨ Sanitize 𝝆1; Desanitize 𝝊^1; Sanitize 𝝆2;\displaystyle A_{1}=\langle\mbox{ Sanitize }\boldsymbol{\rho}_{1};\mbox{ Desanitize }\hat{\boldsymbol{\upsilon}}_{1};\mbox{ Sanitize }\boldsymbol{\rho}_{2};

    Without perpetual retention of state information, a stateful solution restricts a user to a specific action sequence of sanitizing and de-sanitizing in order (such as, A1A_{1}). Moreover, multiple de-sanitization of the same string cannot be supported without perpetual storage of the state information. The issue is exacerbated with multiple users as a stateful solution entails storing separate state information for each user. In contrast, a stateless solution provides the flexibility of supporting arbitrary sequences of user actions (such as, A2A_{2}).

Note that while tateless, conversation with the LLM can be stateful – the LLM is free to maintain a history of all the (sanitized) prompts to better respond to user queries. We illustrate this experimentally in Sec. 5.

4.6 Comparison with Strawman Solutions

We discuss few strawman solutions and why they don’t meet our goals.

Strawman Solution I: Redaction. One intuitive solution is to simply redact all the sensitive tokens from the prompt. While this approach ensures perfect privacy, it severely impacts utility and can sometimes lead to a complete loss of functionality. For example, consider a factual information retrieval task in the context of RAG for financial documents, with the prompt: “Please return all bank accounts with balance greater than $2000.” If the sensitive tokens of the types [Bank A/c] or [Bank Balance] are redacted, the LLM is unable to provide any useful response. Furthermore, any tasks which rely on operations over sensitive information, such as numerical reasoning, would similarly provide little to no utility, even if redacted tokens are replaced by a ”best guess” of a masked language model.

Strawman Solution II: Substitution. Another intuitive solution is to substitute the sensitive tokens with different tokens of the same type via a lookup table. However, there are a couple of issues with this approach. First, such a solution is not stateless as desanitization requires access to these lookup tables and hence, suffers from the drawbacks discussed in Sec. 4.3. The issues are exacerbated here since the size of the lookup table grows linearly with the number of sensitive tokens. Additionally, a new lookup table is required for every user (otherwise the sanitized prompts will leak additional information: this is akin to using the same secret key for multiple users). he other hand, needs access to just a fixed size key per user irrespective of the number or length of prompts.

Strawman Solution III: Suppression. Consider the following sanitization strategy for the tokens of the second category (τII)(\tau_{\texttt{II}}): a numerical token is sanitized by simply setting its kk lowest order digits to 0. The rationale behind this approach is that the LLM’s response is most likely to depend on the higher-order digits, thereby preserving utility while only leaking information about the numerical value at a coarser granularity. However, the key issue with this method is the difficulty in formally quantifying its privacy guarantees. Prior work shows that such ad-hoc approaches are often vulnerable to attacks [36, 31, 75]. In contrast, the mLDP-based approach used in ers a principled way of balancing this privacy/utility trade-off.

Additional Baseline: LLMs assisted obfuscation and deobfuscation. One could also attempt to use a LLM to obfuscate and deobfuscate sensitive information based on rules in the system prompt, and maintaining a state to recover information, such as [91]. However there it is very hard to give any rigorous privacy guarantee for such solutions, because LLM is probabilistic and we don’t have a precise specification of the model.

Comparison with prior work is presented in Sec. 5.2 and Sec. 7.
